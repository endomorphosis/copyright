# The Engravers' Copyright Act 1767

**Citation:** 7 Geo. 3 c. 38  
**Full Title:** *An Act to amend and render more effectual an Act made in the Eighth Year of the Reign of His late Majesty King George the Second, for Encouragement of the Arts of Designing, Engraving, and Etching Historical and other Prints*  
**Royal Assent:** 1767

## Background and Context

Thirty-two years after the original Engravers' Copyright Act of 1735, practical experience with the legislation had revealed a number of limitations and ambiguities. The 1735 Act had been framed narrowly around the specific techniques of engraving and etching, reflecting the print-production methods dominant at the time of its passage. By the 1760s, however, the print trade had diversified significantly. New reproductive techniques—including mezzotint, aquatint, stipple engraving, and various hybrid processes—were in common use, and it was not always clear whether a particular print produced by these newer methods fell within the protection of the 1735 Act.

More significantly, a question had arisen about whether the protection extended to **three-dimensional works** that were reproduced or cast. Models, moulds, and casts were increasingly produced as artistic and decorative objects in their own right, and the print trade shaded into the sculptural trade through the production of medallions, plaques, and other objects with both artistic and commercial value. Whether such objects were "prints" within the meaning of the 1735 Act was doubtful.

The 1767 amendment was Parliament's response to these practical difficulties.

---

## Extension to Works of Similar Nature

The most important substantive provision of the 1767 Act was its extension of the 1735 Act's protection to works **"of a similar nature"** to engravings and prints. The original Act had been drafted by reference to specific techniques; the amendment introduced a broader criterion of functional similarity.

This "similar nature" language was significant because it moved English copyright law away from technique-specific definitions toward a more conceptual approach. Rather than listing protected techniques exhaustively—a list that would inevitably become outdated as new techniques developed—the amendment asked whether a new work was functionally similar to the paradigm cases the original Act had addressed. This functional approach is characteristic of mature copyright law, which typically seeks principles capable of application to technologies not yet invented.

---

## Clarification That Protection Extended to Sculptures and Casts

The 1767 Act explicitly clarified that the protection of the 1735 Act, as amended, extended to **sculptures and casts**. Models produced by sculptors, casts taken from those models, and works that were primarily three-dimensional rather than two-dimensional were brought within the statutory framework.

This clarification was important for several reasons:

- It acknowledged that the creative work deserving protection was not merely the physical surface of a print but the *underlying artistic conception* capable of being reproduced in multiple media.
- It signaled that copyright protection could attach to three-dimensional objects, not just flat works on paper—a principle that would eventually be developed more fully by the Sculpture Copyright Act 1798.
- It suggested that the criterion for copyright protection was *creative originality* and *commercial reproduction*, not the specific physical form in which the work appeared.

In practical terms, the clarification benefited manufacturers of decorative objects—Wedgwood pottery designs, for example, which were produced from sculptural models—by making clear that unauthorized copying of such designs could be challenged under the copyright statutes.

---

## Increased Penalties

The 1767 Act also **increased the monetary penalties** for infringement beyond those set in the 1735 Act. The penalty structure of the original Act had been set at a level appropriate for the 1730s; by the 1760s inflation and the increased profitability of the print trade meant that the penalties no longer provided a meaningful deterrent.

The amendment increased both the per-copy forfeiture and the additional monetary penalties available to the successful complainant. The increased penalties brought print infringement penalties into rough alignment with those available under the Statute of Anne for book copyright infringement.

The forfeiture-of-plates remedy from the 1735 Act was retained and applied equally to the extended categories of works. An infringer who had produced moulds or casts of protected sculptural works could have those moulds and casts forfeited, preventing future reproduction.

---

## Procedural Improvements

Beyond its substantive provisions, the 1767 Act made several procedural improvements to the enforcement mechanism:

- It clarified the **jurisdiction of the courts** for actions under the print copyright statutes, specifying that common-law courts of record (King's Bench, Common Pleas) had jurisdiction without the need to resort to Chancery.
- It improved the **pleading rules** for actions under the Act, reducing the technical difficulties that had made some litigation under the 1735 Act unnecessarily complex.
- It confirmed that the **formality requirement** (engraving the designer's name and date on the plate) applied equally to the newly extended categories of works.

---

## Relationship to Concurrent Legal Developments

The 1767 Act was passed in the same period as the important litigation over common-law copyright that culminated in *Millar v. Taylor* (1769). The timing is not coincidental: the 1760s and early 1770s were a period of intense interest in the legal foundations of copyright. The question of whether copyright rested on statute alone or on deeper common-law foundations was being debated not only in the courts but in Parliament.

The extension and clarification of the print copyright statutes in 1767 can be read as Parliament's implicit endorsement of the statutory approach: rather than relying on common-law principles to protect print designers, Parliament affirmatively extended the statutory framework. This was consistent with the approach that the House of Lords would eventually vindicate in *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774)—that copyright protection required statutory authorization rather than common-law natural right.

---

## Significance

The 1767 Act, though often overshadowed by the major copyright decisions and statutes of its era, has several claims to historical importance:

1. **It introduced the concept of functional similarity as a criterion for copyright protection**, moving English copyright law toward a principled rather than a purely technical approach to subject-matter definition.

2. **It first brought three-dimensional sculptural works within copyright protection**, anticipating the Sculpture Copyright Act 1798 and foreshadowing the eventual extension of copyright to all original artistic works regardless of their dimensional form.

3. **It demonstrated Parliament's willingness to update and improve copyright legislation as practical problems emerged**, establishing the pattern of periodic legislative revision that has characterized copyright law ever since.

4. **It completed the protection of the visual arts trade** that the 1735 Act had begun, ensuring that the full range of reproductive techniques and media then in commercial use was covered by the statutory framework. Together, the 1735 and 1767 Acts gave the English visual arts trade a copyright system as comprehensive as the one the book trade had enjoyed since 1710.

## Primary Source Text

The Engravers' Copyright Act 1767, full title *An Act to amend and render more effectual an Act made in the Eighth Year of the Reign of His late Majesty King George the Second, for Encouragement of the Arts of Designing, Engraving, and Etching Historical and other Prints*, received Royal Assent in 1767. The following are the key operative provisions in modernized spelling.

---

**Engravers' Copyright Act Amendment, 7 Geo. 3 c. 38 (1767)**

**Preamble:**

> Whereas by an Act made in the Eighth Year of the Reign of His late Majesty King George the Second, intitled, An Act for the Encouragement of the Arts of Designing, Engraving, and Etching Historical and other Prints, it was enacted... But whereas the said Act hath been found in some respects defective...

**Section 1 — Extension to Portraits, Maps, and All Other Prints:**

> Be it enacted... That the said recited Act shall be, and is hereby, extended to every Person who shall engrave, etch, or work in Mezzotinto or Chiaro Oscuro, or cause to be engraved, etched, or worked, any Portrait, Conversation, Landscape, or Architecture, Map, Chart, Plan, or any other Print or Prints whatsoever; and the Provisions of the said Act shall apply and extend to all and every such Person and Persons...

**Section 2 — Increased Penalties:**

The 1767 Act increased the monetary penalties available against infringers beyond those specified in the 1735 Act, strengthening the deterrent effect of the legislation and making enforcement more commercially viable for designers whose prints were of relatively modest individual value.

## Legislative History

### Circumstances Prompting the Amendment

By the mid-1760s, print-sellers and artists had accumulated practical experience of the 1735 Act's limitations. The most significant complaints were:

- The Act's coverage was confined to "historical and other prints," language that had been interpreted narrowly to exclude **portraits**, **maps**, **architectural drawings**, and other common categories of prints that did not fit the "historical" description.
- Newer print techniques and formats had developed since 1735 that the Act's wording did not clearly reach.
- The **penalties** in the 1735 Act had proved insufficient to deter well-resourced pirates, who could calculate that the potential fine was outweighed by the profit from unauthorized copying.

### Commons Proceedings

The bill was introduced in the **House of Commons** during the session of 7 Geo. 3 (1766–1767). The **Commons Journals** for that Parliament record the bill's readings. The amendment was not controversial: it was widely accepted that the existing law had gaps and that extending protection to portraits, maps, and other prints served the same policy rationale as the 1735 Act. The bill progressed through its readings and committee stage without significant opposition.

### Lords Stage and Royal Assent

The bill passed the House of Lords without significant amendment and received **Royal Assent in 1767**, becoming law as 7 Geo. 3 c. 38. The Act operated as an amendment to, and extension of, the 1735 Act, which remained in force as the principal statute governing print copyright.

### Historical Context

The 1767 amendment was passed during the same period as the important common-law copyright litigation that would culminate in *Millar v. Taylor* (1769) and *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774). Parliament's willingness to extend and strengthen the statutory framework for print copyright in 1767 is consistent with the broader legislative attitude toward copyright as a creature of statute—an approach ultimately vindicated by the House of Lords in *Donaldson v. Beckett*, which settled that copyright protection depended on statutory authorization rather than common-law natural right.
