# The Star Chamber Decree on Printing (1586)

**Date:** June 23, 1586  
**Issuing Authority:** The Star Chamber, under the presidency of Archbishop John Whitgift (Archbishop of Canterbury)  
**Formal Style:** *A Decree of the Starre-Chamber Concerning Printing*

---

## Background and Context

By the mid-1580s the English press had grown considerably since the Stationers' Company received its charter in 1557. The number of printers had multiplied, presses had spread to the provinces, and the quality of pre-publication review had become uneven and contested. The Privy Council and the ecclesiastical authorities were increasingly alarmed at the volume of contentious religious literature—Puritan polemic on one side, Catholic tracts smuggled from the Continent on the other—that found its way into print despite existing controls.

Archbishop Whitgift, appointed to Canterbury in 1583 and determined to enforce religious conformity, drove a comprehensive overhaul of press regulation. The resulting decree of 1586 was the most systematic statement of press law England had yet seen. It did not merely supplement the 1557 charter; it built on its institutional framework and added a detailed administrative apparatus that would govern the press for the next half-century.

---

## Restriction of Printing to London

The first major structural provision of the 1586 Decree was the **geographical concentration of the press**. With two expressly recognized exceptions, the decree forbade the establishment or operation of printing presses anywhere outside the **City of London and its immediate liberties**.

The two exceptions were the **University of Oxford** and the **University of Cambridge**, each of which had ancient royal privileges authorizing university printing. Even those presses were subject to university oversight and could not operate as commercial ventures competing with the London trade.

Provincial printing—which had flickered into existence in Norwich, York, and a handful of other towns—was effectively extinguished. The rationale was both practical (London-based authorities could monitor the trade) and political (concentrating printers in one place made enforcement far easier).

---

## Limitation of Master Printers

The decree also addressed the proliferation of printers within London itself. It imposed a **cap on the number of master printers** allowed to operate. Existing master printers were to be reduced to a specified number; no new master printers could be admitted until deaths or retirements brought the total below the permitted limit.

The Stationers' Company was made the instrument of enforcement: it maintained the list of authorized master printers and was responsible for ensuring that unlicensed presses did not operate. By controlling entry into the master-printer class, the Company could maintain quality standards while also—inevitably—managing competition in ways that protected established members' commercial interests.

---

## Pre-Publication Licensing by Subject Matter

The heart of the 1586 Decree was its reorganization of **pre-publication licensing**. The requirement that books be approved before printing was not new; it had appeared in royal proclamations and earlier Star Chamber orders going back to the 1530s. What the 1586 Decree added was a systematic allocation of licensing authority by **subject matter**:

| Subject Matter | Licensing Authority |
|---|---|
| Divinity, religion, and church matters | The Archbishop of Canterbury or the Bishop of London |
| Common law and legal matters | The Lord Chief Justice of England and the Lord Chief Justice of Common Pleas |
| Matters of state and government | A Principal Secretary of State |
| All other books | The Bishop of London, or the Archbishop of Canterbury, or their deputies |

This subject-matter allocation reflected the Stuart and Elizabethan state's concept that different spheres of learning fell under distinct jurisdictions: the church governed religious orthodoxy, the law governed legal publications, and the Privy Council governed political discourse. By distributing licensing authority across these institutions, the Crown ensured that no single bottleneck could develop and that books touching sensitive areas received review by experts in the relevant field.

Licensors were expected not merely to approve works on grounds of orthodoxy but also to ensure they did not violate existing law or the interests of existing privilege-holders within the book trade. In practice, licensors wielded very broad discretion.

---

## Registration Requirement in the Stationers' Register

The decree reinforced the requirement that **all licensed books be entered in the Stationers' Register** before printing. Registration served as evidence both that the book had been duly licensed and that the registering stationer held the exclusive trade right to print it.

The two-stage structure was now codified:

1. **Substantive approval** — the licensing officer approved the content.
2. **Proprietary registration** — the stationer entered the approved work in the Register, securing the "copy."

A book that had been licensed but not registered was in an anomalous position; a book that had been registered but not licensed was unlawful and subject to seizure. The decree insisted on both steps, making them mutually reinforcing.

---

## Search and Seizure

The 1586 Decree substantially expanded the search and seizure powers first granted in the 1557 charter. The Master and Wardens of the Stationers' Company were empowered to **enter and search any premises**—printing houses, bookshops, warehouses, even private homes—where they had reason to believe unlicensed or otherwise unlawful books were stored or produced. They could **seize such books** without prior judicial process and bring them before the ecclesiastical or civil authorities for disposal.

In addition, the decree authorized seizure of printing equipment used to produce unlicensed works. The prospect of losing one's press—a capital investment of considerable size—was a powerful deterrent.

---

## Penalties

Penalties under the decree were severe:

- **Unlicensed printing:** Imprisonment and permanent disablement from future practice in the printing trade.
- **Failure to register:** Forfeiture of the edition and a fine.
- **Printing seditious or heretical matter:** Referral to the ecclesiastical courts or the Star Chamber for punishment that could include mutilation or imprisonment.

The Star Chamber itself retained jurisdiction over the most serious offenses, and its punishments were notoriously harsh. Printers who produced politically sensitive unlicensed material risked not just professional ruin but physical punishment.

---

## Significance: The Two-Stage Structure Codified

The 1586 Decree is significant in copyright history primarily because it **codified the two-stage structure** that would persist into the Statute of Anne:

- **Substantive approval** (licensing) was a regulatory requirement protecting the public interest in controlling the content of printed material.
- **Proprietary registration** was a private-law mechanism protecting the trade interest of the registering stationer.

These two functions served different masters and rested on different justifications, yet they were procedurally fused: the same act of registration that documented licensing also created the trade right. This fusion would become a source of tension as arguments about copyright shifted from guild monopoly toward authors' natural rights.

The decree also matters for what it reveals about the **limits of the Register** as a copyright instrument. The right it protected was purely a *trade* right enforceable among guild members. Authors continued to have no recognized interest. And the right was practically unlimited in duration: a stationer who had entered a work in the Register held it for as long as the Company's jurisdiction could be maintained—potentially forever. It was this potentially perpetual, guild-based monopoly that the Statute of Anne would eventually displace by substituting a statutory term of fixed duration vested initially in the author.

## Primary Source Text

The following reproduces the principal provisions of the Star Chamber Decree of 1586, in modernized spelling. The decree was issued under the authority of the Star Chamber (the Court of the Privy Council sitting in its judicial capacity) and had the force of law without being a parliamentary statute.

---

**A Decree of the Starre-Chamber Concerning Printing, 23 June 1586**

**Restriction of presses to London (with exceptions for the Universities):**

> Item, that no printer of books do keep any press or presses of printing, or use the art or mystery of printing, in any place or places but only in the City of London, or the suburbs thereof; except one press in the University of Cambridge and one press in the University of Oxford.

**Limitation on the number of master printers:**

> Item, that no person shall keep above one press of printing at one time; and that the number of master printers shall be reduced and brought to such a small number as by the Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London shall be thought convenient.

**Pre-publication licensing by subject matter:**

> Item, that no person shall imprint or cause to be imprinted, or shall bring or procure to be brought into this realm imprinted, any book, work, or copy, against the form and meaning of any ordinance, prohibition, or commandment, contained or to be contained in any the statutes or laws of this realm, or in any injunction, letters patent, or ordinances set forth or to be set forth by the Queen's most excellent Majesty's grant, commission, or authority: And that no person shall bring, or cause to be brought, from beyond the seas into this realm, any manner of English books, ballads, or other works printed beyond the seas, to be sold here, unless it be first seen, examined, and allowed; except books in Latin, or Greek, or other tongue, being approved, or to be approved, by the said Archbishop of Canterbury and Bishop of London, or the one of them. And that every book concerning the laws of this realm shall first be allowed by the Lord Chief Justice and Lord Chief Baron, or one of them; and if the book touch matters of state or the common weal, then by the said Secretaries or one of them; and if it be a book concerning heraldry, titles of honor and arms, or otherwise concerning the office of the Earl Marshal, then to be allowed by the Earl Marshal.

**Registration requirement:**

> Item, that no person shall imprint, or cause to be imprinted, any book, work, or copy, without it be first entered in the Register Book of the Stationers' Company of the City of London; and that no printing shall be done but in open shops, that the wardens of the Stationers' Company may see the same.

**Search and seizure:**

> Item, that the Wardens of the Stationers' Company for the time being, or any two of the journeymen printers to be assigned by the said Wardens, shall make search in all workhouses, shops, warehouses, and other places of printers, booksellers, and such as bring books from beyond the seas, as well within the liberties as without: and all books found to be printed contrary to these orders shall be brought to the Hall of the said Company and there defaced, or the bookseller or printer found guilty thereof shall be imprisoned for the space of six months.
