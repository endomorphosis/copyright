# Millar v. Taylor (1769)

**Citation:** (1769) 4 Burr. 2303; 98 Eng. Rep. 201  
**Court:** Court of King's Bench  
**Decision:** Three to one in favour of the plaintiff  
**Judges:** Lord Chief Justice Lord Mansfield; Justice Willes; Justice Aston (majority); Justice Yates (dissent)

---

## Background and Historical Context

*Millar v. Taylor* was decided at the height of a prolonged controversy over whether the Statute of Anne had *replaced* the common-law rights of authors and publishers in their works or had merely *supplemented* them with additional statutory remedies. The controversy had simmered since the Act's passage in 1710, because the London booksellers—who had invested heavily in the assumption that their registered "copies" represented perpetual property rights—faced an existential commercial crisis if those rights evaporated at the end of the statutory terms.

By the late 1760s the twenty-one-year transitional term granted to existing books by the Statute of Anne had long since expired for most works, and the fourteen-year initial terms were expiring for works published after 1710. Provincial printers and, most threatening of all, Edinburgh booksellers led by Alexander Donaldson were freely reprinting works whose statutory terms had expired, selling them at prices far below those charged by the London trade. The London booksellers argued that this reprinting was unlawful because a perpetual common-law copyright continued to protect their copies even after the statutory term had run. *Millar v. Taylor* was the test case designed to resolve that argument.

---

## The Facts

**Andrew Millar** was one of the leading London booksellers of the mid-eighteenth century. He had acquired by purchase the copyright in *The Seasons*, a celebrated poem by **James Thomson** (1700–1748), under the Statute of Anne. The poem had been published in installments between 1726 and 1730, with *The Seasons* collected edition in 1730. The statutory copyright under the Statute of Anne had been duly registered and had run its fourteen-year initial term, with a renewal term (Thomson having died in 1748, the renewal vested in the proprietors rather than the author).

**Robert Taylor**, a printer, produced and sold an edition of *The Seasons* after the statutory term had expired, on the theory that the work was now in the public domain and freely available to any printer.

Millar brought an action for infringement. The central legal question was not disputed as a matter of fact but as a matter of law: **did any right in the work survive the expiry of the Statute of Anne's term?**

---

## The Legal Question

The question presented to the King's Bench was: **Did a perpetual common-law copyright in published literary works exist at common law, independently of and alongside the statutory copyright created by the Statute of Anne?**

This question had two sub-parts:

1. **Did a common-law copyright in unpublished manuscripts exist before the Statute of Anne?** (Almost everyone agreed it did: an author's unpublished manuscript was protected by the common law against unauthorized copying.)

2. **Did publication extinguish that common-law right, leaving only the statutory remedy?** Or did the common-law right *survive* publication and *continue* after the statutory term expired, giving the author (and his assignees) a perpetual right against all other printers?

---

## The Majority: Mansfield, Willes, and Aston JJ

All three majority justices held that a common-law copyright in published works existed and was not extinguished by the Statute of Anne.

### Lord Chief Justice Mansfield

Mansfield's opinion was the most fully reasoned. He argued:

- **Natural right:** An author's work was the product of his mental labor. Just as the law recognized property rights arising from physical labor, it should recognize property rights arising from intellectual labor. This natural right did not depend on statute; it was a principle the common law would recognize independently.

- **The Statute of Anne did not extinguish common-law rights:** The Statute provided new remedies—the penny-per-sheet penalty, a specific form of action—but it did not expressly abrogate existing common-law rights. Statutes in derogation of existing rights are to be construed narrowly; if Parliament had intended to extinguish the perpetual common-law copyright, it would have said so clearly.

- **Publication did not abandon the right:** An author who published a work for sale was not abandoning all property in it; he was exercising a particular form of exploitation while retaining the underlying property right. The act of publication was analogous to selling copies of a manuscript: the physical copies were transferred, but the right to make further copies remained with the author.

Mansfield's opinion was a powerful synthesis of natural rights philosophy and common-law reasoning. It drew on Lockean property theory and on analogies from real property and trade secret law. For a brief period—five years—it was the law of England.

### Justice Willes

Willes agreed with Mansfield but grounded his opinion more directly in the history of the Stationers' Company and the Licensing Acts. He argued that the pre-1710 "copy" right, though originating in guild practice, had long been recognized by the courts as a genuine property right, and that the Statute of Anne was best understood as *building on* that existing right rather than replacing it.

### Justice Aston

Aston similarly agreed that common-law copyright existed and survived the Statute of Anne, though his opinion added less to the majority's reasoning than those of Mansfield and Willes.

---

## The Dissent: Justice Yates

Justice Yates dissented vigorously and at length, producing an opinion that many later commentators have regarded as more persuasive than the majority's.

### Yates's Principal Arguments

- **Property rights in ideas are inherently different from property rights in physical things.** Physical property is excludable and rivalrous: if I possess a piece of land, you cannot. Ideas are non-excludable and non-rivalrous: if I know a poem by heart, your knowing it too does not diminish my knowledge. The common law's traditional property concepts were designed for physical things and could not simply be transposed to intangible works.

- **Publication was a dedication to the public.** When an author published a work for sale, he surrendered the exclusive possession that was the foundation of common-law property in unpublished manuscripts. The natural right that protected a manuscript in the author's desk did not survive the voluntary act of making it public.

- **The Statute of Anne represented Parliament's considered judgment** about the proper balance between the author's interest and the public interest. Parliament had decided that the appropriate term of protection was fourteen years (or twenty-one for existing works). If courts recognized a perpetual common-law copyright alongside the statute, they would be substituting their judgment for Parliament's on the most important policy question the statute addressed.

- **Monopoly in perpetuity was harmful.** Yates was influenced by the emerging free-trade critique of commercial monopolies. A perpetual copyright in a poem or treatise was economically similar to a perpetual patent in an invention: it removed the work from the competitive market forever and concentrated the fruits of an author's labor in a small number of London booksellers rather than distributing them to the reading public through price competition.

Yates's dissent anticipated the reasoning of the House of Lords in *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774) and is regarded by many copyright historians as the more legally sound analysis.

---

## Outcome and Immediate Effect

The majority held in favor of Millar, recognizing a perpetual common-law copyright in published works. For the period between 1769 and 1774, this was the law of England: once an author or his assignee established a copyright in a work under the Statute of Anne, that copyright continued in perpetuity at common law even after the statutory term expired.

Millar died in 1768, before the judgment was handed down. His executors sold his copyrights—including the copyright in *The Seasons*—to a syndicate of London booksellers including Thomas Beckett. It was Beckett who then faced the challenge from Alexander Donaldson, the Edinburgh reprinter, in the litigation that became *Donaldson v. Beckett*.

---

## Significance

*Millar v. Taylor* is a pivotal case in copyright history for several reasons:

1. **It articulated the natural rights argument for copyright** more fully and powerfully than any previous English judicial opinion. Mansfield's reasoning drew on Locke, on Roman law, and on the emerging philosophy of intellectual property that would influence Romantic theories of authorship. Even though *Millar* was ultimately overruled in *Donaldson*, the natural rights argument it articulated has never entirely disappeared from copyright discourse.

2. **It created a brief period of perpetual copyright** in English law, during which the London booksellers' claim to monopoly rights seemed vindicated. The reversal of this position five years later in *Donaldson* was all the more dramatic for this brief triumph.

3. **Yates J's dissent** provided the intellectual foundation for the House of Lords' decision in *Donaldson*. The argument that publication is a dedication to the public, and that Parliament's fixed statutory term was the intended limit of copyright protection, was vindicated by the Lords even though it had been rejected in King's Bench.

4. **Together with *Donaldson v. Beckett***, *Millar v. Taylor* defined the fundamental nature of copyright in the common-law tradition: it is a statutory right, not a natural right, and its term is fixed and limited, not perpetual. This principle has remained foundational in British and American copyright law ever since.
