# The Statute of Anne (1710)

**Citation:** 8 Anne c. 19  
**Full Title:** *An Act for the Encouragement of Learning, by Vesting the Copies of Printed Books in the Authors or Purchasers of such Copies, during the Times therein mentioned*  
**Royal Assent:** April 5, 1710  
**Effective Date:** April 10, 1710

---

## Introduction

The Statute of Anne is the **first true copyright statute in the history of the world**. For the first time in any legal system, a legislative enactment recognized copyright as a distinct legal right, defined its scope and duration by statute, and—most revolutionary of all—vested that right initially in the *author* of a work rather than in the publisher or guild that printed it.

The Act emerged from fifteen years of pressure on Parliament following the lapse of the Licensing Act in 1695 (see the separate entry for that event). The London Stationers and their bookseller allies had repeatedly sought legislation to restore their trade rights in the books they had registered under the old system. They succeeded in 1710, but the statute they obtained was shaped by the competing argument that it was *authors* who deserved protection, and by a parliamentary determination to limit the duration of the monopoly rather than restore the potentially perpetual right the Stationers had enjoyed.

---

## The Two-Tier Term Structure

The Act established two distinct copyright terms, depending on when the work was first published:

### (a) Existing Books: Twenty-One Year Term

For books **already in print on the effective date of April 10, 1710**, the Act granted a copyright term of **twenty-one years** from that date. This term was granted to the *current owner of the copy*—in practice, the London bookseller who held the registered "copy" under the old Licensing Act system. The rationale was transitional: existing investments in printed works deserved some protection, but the duration was fixed and not perpetual.

This twenty-one-year term for existing works provoked one of the most important legal controversies of the century. The London booksellers argued that at the end of the twenty-one years their copies reverted to them by virtue of a pre-existing *common-law* copyright that the statute had not extinguished. This argument eventually reached the courts in *Millar v. Taylor* (1769) and the House of Lords in *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774).

### (b) New Books: Fourteen-Year Initial Term with Fourteen-Year Renewal

For books **first published after April 10, 1710**, the Act granted:

- An **initial term of fourteen years** from the date of first publication, provided the work was registered before publication.
- A **renewal term of a further fourteen years** if, at the expiry of the initial term, the *author* was still living. The renewal term vested in the author, not the original publisher, even if the author had previously assigned the copyright. This provision, which gave the author a second chance at the work if the publisher's term expired during the author's lifetime, was a remarkable statutory innovation—it recognized the author's continuing interest in a work even after sale.

The fourteen-year term was not chosen arbitrarily: it corresponded to the maximum term of a patent of monopoly under the Statute of Monopolies 1624 (21 Jac. 1 c. 3), which Parliament and the courts had already accepted as a legitimate monopoly term for inventions. By using the same term, Parliament signaled that copyright was analogous to patent—a limited statutory monopoly rather than a perpetual natural right.

---

## The Author as Initial Right-Holder

The most revolutionary feature of the Statute of Anne was the provision that the copyright in a new book vested initially in **the author or authors** of the work, and only subsequently passed to publishers or other assignees by contract.

This was a complete inversion of the Stationers' system, under which the registering *bookseller* held the "copy" regardless of the author's wishes or interests. The Act stated in terms that "the author of any book or books already printed, who hath not transferred to any other the copy or copies of such book or books," and the author of any book to be printed after the Act, should have the copyright benefit under the statute.

In practice, authors in 1710 were economically dependent on the bookselling trade and typically assigned their copyrights immediately upon publication. The assignee-bookseller then held the statutory term in the same way as a stationer had held the old "copy." But the conceptual change was profound: copyright was now *derived from the author* and *transferred by the author's act*. It was not a guild right that accrued by the act of registration.

This author-as-originator principle became the template for every subsequent copyright statute in the common-law world, including the US Copyright Act of 1790.

---

## Registration with the Stationers' Company

Despite vesting the right in the author, the Act preserved **registration with the Stationers' Company** as a precondition for bringing an action under the statute. Section 2 provided that no person should be entitled to the benefit of the Act unless the title of the book was registered in the Register Book of the Stationers' Company before publication.

Registration thus remained an important practical step, but its character had changed: it was now a *formality required to activate a statutory right* rather than the constitutive act that *created* the right. The right came from authorship; registration was required to enforce it.

This ambiguity between right and remedy persisted throughout the eighteenth century and contributed to the courts' uncertainty in the common-law copyright cases of the 1760s and 1770s.

---

## Deposit of Nine Copies for Libraries

The Act continued the statutory deposit requirement established by the 1662 Licensing Act, requiring the printer to deliver **nine copies** of each registered book before publication to the Stationers' Company. These copies were to be distributed to specified libraries:

- The **Royal Library** (later the British Museum Library, now the British Library)
- The **Bodleian Library**, University of Oxford
- The **Cambridge University Library**
- The **four Scottish university libraries** (St Andrews, Glasgow, Aberdeen, Edinburgh)
- The library of **Sion College**, London
- The **Faculty of Advocates' Library**, Edinburgh (added in recognition of the Acts of Union 1707)

The deposit requirement served both as a public library service and as a mechanism for ensuring that registered works could be examined and verified. Failure to deposit did not void the copyright, but it made the work unregistered and therefore unenforceable under the Act—a significant practical consequence.

The nine-copy deposit (later reduced by subsequent statutes) is the ancestor of modern legal deposit requirements in the United Kingdom and was the direct model for the deposit requirements in the US Copyright Act of 1790.

---

## Price Regulation

One of the most unusual provisions of the Statute of Anne—one that has no successor in modern copyright law—was its **price regulation mechanism**. Section 4 provided that if the proprietor of a copyright book set the price at a level that was deemed "unreasonable and too high," any person could complain to:

- The **Archbishop of Canterbury**
- The **Lord Chancellor**
- The **Bishop of London**
- The **Lord Chief Justice of the King's Bench**
- The **Lord Chief Justice of Common Pleas**
- The **Lord Chief Baron of the Exchequer**
- The **Vice-Chancellors of Oxford or Cambridge**

On receiving a complaint, the designated officer was empowered to set a **reasonable price** for the book, which the proprietor was then required to observe.

This provision reflected Parliament's concern that the copyright monopoly might be used to charge excessive prices for books necessary for learning. In practice, the price regulation mechanism was almost never invoked—the commercial dynamics of the book trade generally kept prices competitive—but its presence in the Act signals Parliament's understanding that copyright was a *limited monopoly granted for a public purpose*, not a property right exercisable without constraint.

---

## Prohibition on Importing Foreign Reprints

Continuing the tradition established by the 1637 Star Chamber Decree and the 1662 Licensing Act, the Statute of Anne **prohibited the importation of foreign reprints** of books first published in England under the statutory protection. Any such books imported without the copyright proprietor's consent were subject to forfeiture and the importer to a penalty.

This provision gave the copyright monopoly effective territorial scope: not only could unauthorized domestic reprints be suppressed, but competing editions printed abroad—whether in Amsterdam, Dublin, or Edinburgh—could not be imported.

---

## Forfeiture and Penalties for Infringement

The Act's enforcement provisions provided that any person who printed, reprinted, or imported a copyright work without the proprietor's consent was subject to:

- **Forfeiture of the infringing copies** to the copyright proprietor.
- A **penalty of one penny per sheet** found in the infringer's possession, half to go to the Crown and half to the complainant.

These were relatively modest penalties compared to the severe punishments available under the old Star Chamber system, but they gave copyright proprietors a practical remedy in the common-law courts. Actions under the Act were tried by jury, and the Act set out specific procedural requirements designed to make enforcement accessible without requiring resort to the Court of Chancery.

---

## Savings Clause for University Printing Privileges

The Act included a **savings clause** expressly preserving the existing printing privileges of the Universities of Oxford and Cambridge. These ancient privileges, granted by royal charter and confirmed by statute, allowed the universities to print and sell books in competition with the London trade. The savings clause ensured that the Statute of Anne did not inadvertently extinguish these privileges, which the universities regarded as vital to their educational mission.

---

## Universities' Right to Import Foreign Books

A separate provision authorized the two universities and the four colleges then recognized as principal academic institutions to **import books printed abroad** for their own use, notwithstanding the general anti-importation provision. This exception recognized that foreign scholarly works were essential to academic life and that the anti-importation prohibition should not prevent libraries and scholars from obtaining foreign editions of works not published in England.

---

## Significance

The Statute of Anne's historical significance is difficult to overstate:

1. **It vested copyright in the author as initial right-holder**, establishing the author-as-originator principle that underlies all subsequent copyright law.

2. **It introduced a fixed statutory term** in place of the potentially perpetual guild right, establishing the principle that copyright monopolies must be time-limited.

3. **It preserved registration and deposit** from the Licensing Act tradition, transforming them from instruments of censorship into formalities of a property rights system.

4. **It created a price regulation mechanism** that acknowledged copyright as a limited monopoly serving a public purpose.

5. **It established the anti-importation principle** as a statutory entitlement of the copyright proprietor.

6. **It directly influenced the US Copyright Act of 1790**, which replicated the Statute of Anne's structure almost exactly: an initial fourteen-year term with a fourteen-year renewal if the author was still living, registration as a precondition for suit, deposit of copies for public libraries, and prohibition on importing foreign reprints. The framers of the US Constitution's Copyright Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 8) and the drafters of the 1790 Act worked consciously within the framework the Statute of Anne had established.

7. **It generated the great common-law copyright controversy** that culminated in *Millar v. Taylor* (1769) and *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774), whose resolution established that copyright is a creature of statute rather than common law—a foundational principle that remains in force today.
