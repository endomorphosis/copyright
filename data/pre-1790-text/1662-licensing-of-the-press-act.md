# Licensing of the Press Act 1662

**Short Title:** Licensing of the Press Act 1662  
**Citation:** 14 Car. 2 c. 33  
**Full Title:** *An Act for preventing the frequent Abuses in printing seditious treasonable and unlicensed Bookes and Pamphlets and for regulating of Printing and Printing Presses*  
**Royal Assent:** May 10, 1662  
**Initial Expiry:** Designed with a two-year sunset; repeatedly renewed

---

## Background and Context

When Charles II was restored to the English throne in 1660, one of his government's most urgent practical concerns was the regulation of the press. The intervening years of Civil War and Interregnum had seen an unprecedented explosion of unlicensed printing. The Long Parliament's Licensing Order of June 14, 1643 had attempted to re-establish pre-publication licensing on a parliamentary basis, but it had been imperfect, contested, and intermittently enforced. By 1660 the London book trade was in a state of regulatory chaos.

The first two years of the Restoration were occupied with more urgent constitutional business. The Licensing of the Press Act 1662 was the Cavalier Parliament's comprehensive response, enacted in the first full session of what would prove a long-lived Parliament. It drew directly on the framework of the 1637 Star Chamber Decree, translating that prerogative instrument into statutory form while tightening its provisions in several respects.

The Act was presented to Parliament partly as a measure to suppress sedition and heresy, and partly as a measure to restore orderly trade to the Stationers' Company and the London book industry, which had suffered considerably from unlicensed competition during the Interregnum. Both justifications reflected genuine legislative concern.

---

## Principal Provisions

### Pre-Publication Licensing

The Act's central requirement was that **no book, pamphlet, or paper could be printed or published without prior approval by an authorized licensor**. The system of licensing by subject matter, which the Star Chamber Decrees had developed, was continued and placed on statutory footing:

- **Matters of law:** Licensed by the Lord Chancellor, Lord Keeper, or one of the Chief Justices or Chief Baron of the Exchequer.
- **Matters of history or affairs of state:** Licensed by one of the Principal Secretaries of State.
- **Matters of heraldry:** Licensed by the Earl Marshal.
- **All other books** (including divinity, philosophy, science, and literature): Licensed by the Archbishop of Canterbury, the Bishop of London, the Vice-Chancellors of Oxford or Cambridge (for books published at those universities), or their deputies.

The licensor was to read the work before approving it and to certify that it contained nothing contrary to the Christian faith, the doctrine and discipline of the Church of England, or the laws and government of the realm. A signed testimonial of approval was required before the Stationers' Company would register the work.

### Registration with the Stationers' Company

Consistent with the pre-Restoration regime, the Act **required registration in the Stationers' Register** as a condition of lawful printing. The registered stationer acquired the exclusive right to print the work—the "copy"—enforceable against other members of the Company. Unregistered printing of a licensed work was still an offense, though a less serious one than unlicensed printing.

### Restriction of Presses

The Act reaffirmed the restriction of printing presses to the **City of London and its liberties**, the two universities, and the town of York (a minor concession). No new printing houses could be established without authorization. Existing houses were to be registered with the Stationers' Company. The number of master printers and type-founders was to be kept to a level determined by the Master and Wardens.

### Anti-Importation Provision

The Act carried forward the innovation of the 1637 Star Chamber Decree by **prohibiting the importation of foreign-printed copies of books first printed in England** under a license and entered in the Stationers' Register. Any such copies imported were subject to seizure and forfeiture. The importer was also subject to a monetary penalty.

This provision was commercially significant. It extended the effective scope of the registered stationer's trade right to the borders of the kingdom, preventing Continental printers from competing in the English market with cheaper editions of English-licensed books.

### Search and Seizure

The Act authorized the Stationers' Company's officers and the messengers of the press (officers of the Secretary of State) to **search any premises** where unlicensed printing was suspected. They could seize unauthorized presses, type, and books. They could arrest persons found in the act of unlicensed printing. Seized materials were forfeit; the presses could be destroyed.

### Deposit of Nine Copies for Libraries

One of the Act's most important long-term contributions was its **statutory deposit requirement**. The printer was required to deliver **nine copies** of every printed book to the Stationers' Company before publication. These copies were to be distributed as follows:

- One copy to the **Royal Library** (now the British Library)
- One copy to the **Bodleian Library**, Oxford
- One copy to the **Cambridge University Library**
- One copy to the library of **Sion College**, London
- One copy each to the libraries of the **four Scottish universities** (St Andrews, Glasgow, Aberdeen, Edinburgh)

The deposit requirement served multiple purposes: it maintained a record of everything published, it supplied public and university libraries with current publications, and it reinforced the registration system by ensuring physical copies were available for examination.

This nine-copy deposit is the direct ancestor of the modern legal deposit system in the United Kingdom, still in force today, and was the model for the deposit requirements in the Statute of Anne (1710) and the early US copyright acts.

## History of Renewals and Lapses (1662–1695)

The 1662 Act was not conceived as permanent legislation. It contained a sunset clause, and its continuation depended on successive acts of Parliament:

- **1662:** Initial enactment.
- **1664:** First renewal for two years.
- **1665:** Renewed until the end of the next parliamentary session following the end of the current session.
- **1679:** Lapsed when Parliament was prorogued; no renewal act was passed. During this period (1679–1685), the press was briefly free of statutory licensing. The government resorted to the common-law offense of seditious libel as its primary instrument of press control.
- **1685:** Renewed under James II.
- **1692:** Lapsed again as Parliament did not renew it in time.
- **1693:** Renewed for two years.
- **1695:** Parliament declined to renew the Act. It expired on May 3, 1695, and was never revived.

The Act thus had a discontinuous history of about 25 years of actual operation across a period of 33 years. Its lapses were as important as its enactments: each lapse demonstrated that the book trade and the press could function, after a fashion, without pre-publication censorship, and each renewal showed that the political class still desired the mechanism of substantive control when it could be obtained.

---

## The 1695 Lapse: Why Parliament Let It Die

When the 1693 renewal came up for reconsideration in the session of 1694–1695, both Houses of Parliament considered amendments to the Act. The **House of Commons declined to renew** it, and it permanently expired. The reasons given in the formal Commons objections were almost entirely *commercial and legal rather than libertarian*:

- The Act created monopolies in the book trade that were economically harmful.
- Its penalties were poorly calibrated.
- It failed to protect English booksellers against the Irish and Scottish reprinting trade.
- It benefited the Stationers' Company disproportionately at the expense of other traders.

John Locke, who advised the Commons on the matter through his memorandum of 1693–1694, added philosophical objections: the grant of perpetual monopolies over particular works was an unjustifiable restraint of trade and learning; any copyright term, if one were to exist, should be of limited and fixed duration; and pre-publication censorship was itself an objectionable form of prior restraint.

## Significance for Copyright History

The 1662 Act has four principal claims to importance in copyright history:

1. **It codified registration as a statutory prerequisite for trade protection.** By placing the registration system on a parliamentary foundation rather than a prerogative or guild basis, it transformed the Stationers' "copy" from an internal guild right into a statutory entitlement. This shift in legal foundation opened the way for Parliament later to reshape the right itself.

2. **It established statutory deposit as a recognized obligation of printers.** The nine-copy deposit requirement became the template for all subsequent deposit legislation in both Britain and the United States.

3. **It gave statutory force to the anti-importation provision.** The 1637 Decree's anti-importation rule was now a statutory rule, enforceable in the common-law courts as well as by the Company's own machinery.

4. **Its lapse in 1695 created the vacuum that produced the Statute of Anne.** When the Act expired, the Stationers' Company lost both its statutory licensing monopoly and its exclusive trade rights in registered copies. The Company's subsequent lobbying for parliamentary protection of its investments—combined with the emerging argument that authors rather than stationers deserved protection—produced the Statute of Anne in 1710, which transformed copyright from a guild trade right into a statutory right initially vesting in the author. The 1662 Act is thus the immediate predecessor from which the first true copyright statute directly grew.
