# The Engravers' Copyright Act 1735 ("Hogarth's Act")

**Citation:** 8 Geo. 2 c. 13  
**Short Title:** Engravers' Copyright Act 1735; popularly known as **"Hogarth's Act"**  
**Full Title:** *An Act for the Encouragement of the Arts of Designing, Engraving and Etching Historical and other Prints, by vesting the Properties thereof in the Inventors or Engravers during the Time therein mentioned*  
**Royal Assent:** June 25, 1735

---

## Background and Context

When the Statute of Anne was enacted in 1710, its subject matter was limited to **books** in the conventional sense—printed texts. It made no provision for visual works: prints, engravings, maps, charts, or other graphic productions. This omission was commercially significant. The print trade in early eighteenth-century England was substantial and growing rapidly, fueled by public appetite for pictorial news, decorative prints, architectural drawings, and illustrations of all kinds.

The most famous figure in the campaign for print protection was **William Hogarth** (1697–1764), the painter and printmaker whose series of moralistic narrative engravings—*A Rake's Progress* (1735), *Marriage A-la-Mode* (1745), *Industry and Idleness* (1747), and others—were enormously popular and enormously pirated. Hogarth had watched his designs reproduced by cheap copyists almost immediately upon publication, with no legal remedy available. The Statute of Anne did not apply; there was no registration system for prints; and the common law afforded no protection for visual works.

Hogarth and his fellow printmakers organized a campaign to persuade Parliament to extend statutory protection to the print trade. Hogarth himself lobbied Members of Parliament directly, and his artistic reputation gave the campaign a public face that purely commercial petitions would have lacked. The Act that resulted bears his name by popular tradition.

---

## Provisions of the Act

### Extension of Protection to Original Prints and Engravings

The central provision of the 1735 Act extended copyright protection to **original prints and engravings**. Any person who invented or designed an original print—whether produced by engraving, etching, mezzotint, or any similar technique—was granted the exclusive right to print, sell, and distribute that print for the statutory term.

The Act required that the print be **original**: copies of paintings or drawings by other artists, or mechanical reproductions of existing works, were not intended to receive protection. The requirement of originality anticipated the modern concept of creative authorship as the basis of copyright, though the Act did not use that language.

### Fourteen-Year Term

The term of protection was **fourteen years** from the date of first publication—matching the initial term for new books under the Statute of Anne. This was a deliberate choice: by using the same term Parliament signaled that print protection was conceptually analogous to book copyright, a statutory monopoly of fixed and limited duration.

There was no renewal term equivalent to the Statute of Anne's fourteen-year renewal available to authors still living. If the designer of a print was still alive at the end of fourteen years, the print passed into the public domain. This asymmetry between the print and book regimes would not be corrected for many decades.

### Requirement to Engrave Name and Publication Date

A distinctive formality requirement of the 1735 Act was the obligation to **engrave the name of the designer or proprietor and the date of first publication on the plate** from which the print was produced. This requirement served as a form of registration notice: any person examining a print could see its origin and date and determine whether the statutory term was still running.

Failure to comply with the engraving requirement was a bar to action under the statute. A print without the required inscription could not be protected under the Act, though whether it had any other legal protection was uncertain.

This requirement of notice on the work itself was analogous to the modern copyright notice requirement (© symbol, name, year) that survived in US law until the Berne Convention Implementation Act of 1988.

### Forfeiture of Plates and Prints

For infringement—defined as printing, reprinting, engraving, etching, or selling an unauthorized copy of a protected print—the Act provided:

- **Forfeiture of all infringing copies** to the proprietor.
- **Forfeiture of the plates or other means of reproduction** used to make the infringing copies.
- A **monetary penalty** payable to the proprietor.

The forfeiture of plates was a particularly effective remedy in the print trade: unlike a book, where the infringing copies themselves were the main concern, a print's productive apparatus—the copper or iron plate—was the key capital asset. Forfeiting the plate prevented further infringement without any need for additional legal proceedings.

## William Hogarth and the Campaign for the Act

Hogarth's personal role in securing the Act was widely recognized at the time and has been memorialized in his popular title as "Hogarth's Act." His involvement is worth noting in detail because it illustrates a recurring pattern in copyright history: **the expansion of copyright subject matter driven by creators who experience the inadequacy of existing protection**.

Hogarth's series *A Rake's Progress* (1735) was almost simultaneously published and pirated. By organizing a petition and lobbying campaign, Hogarth and his colleagues turned a grievance about specific commercial harm into a principle of general legislative policy. Parliament responded not by protecting Hogarth specifically but by creating a general rule for all print designers—a rule that happened to benefit Hogarth immediately.

Hogarth's involvement in the campaign also shaped the Act's requirement of originality. His argument was that the *designer's* creative labor deserved protection, not merely the labor of mechanical reproduction. A copyist who reproduced an existing painting as an engraving was not, in Hogarth's view, the kind of creative person the law should reward. This argument—that copyright protects *original creative work*, not merely labor—was an important step in the development of the concept of authorship that now underlies copyright law everywhere.

## Significance

The Engravers' Copyright Act 1735 has four principal claims to importance in copyright history:

1. **It was the first copyright protection for visual works.** The Statute of Anne had covered books; the 1735 Act extended copyright into an entirely new domain—graphic art. This was the first step in the long process of expanding copyright subject matter from texts to all creative works, a process that continued through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries and now encompasses photographs, films, sound recordings, software, and architectural works.

2. **It expanded copyright beyond the book trade's institutional framework.** The Stationers' Company and its Register were not involved in the print protection system; there was no equivalent guild for printmakers. The 1735 Act created copyright protection for a trade that had no pre-existing regulatory infrastructure, suggesting that copyright was becoming a general principle of creative property rather than a specific incident of the book trade's guild organization.

3. **It established the fourteen-year term as the standard copyright term.** By using the same term as the Statute of Anne, the 1735 Act reinforced the emerging consensus that fourteen years was an appropriate duration for a copyright monopoly. This consensus directly influenced American copyright law: the US Copyright Act of 1790 also used a fourteen-year term with a fourteen-year renewal.

4. **It was a model for subsequent expansion of copyright subject matter.** The Acts protecting sculptures (1798), dramatic works (1833), lectures (1835), and musical compositions (1882) all followed the structural template of the 1735 Act—a fixed term, originality as a precondition, formality requirements for enforcement, and specific remedies for infringement. The 1735 Act thus established not just print protection but the *legislative method* by which copyright was extended to new categories of creative work.
