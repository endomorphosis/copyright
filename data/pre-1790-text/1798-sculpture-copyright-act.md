# The Sculpture Copyright Act 1798

**Citation:** 38 Geo. 3 c. 71  
**Full Title:** *An Act for encouraging the Art of making new Models and Casts of Busts, and other Things therein mentioned*  
**Royal Assent:** July 2, 1798

---

## Background and Context

By the closing decade of the eighteenth century, English copyright law had developed into a collection of overlapping statutes, each protecting a specific category of creative work:

- **Books** and literary works: Statute of Anne 1710 (8 Anne c. 19)
- **Prints and engravings** (two-dimensional visual works): Engravers' Acts of 1735, 1767, and 1777

One major category of creative work remained without clear statutory protection: **three-dimensional artistic works**—sculptures, models, casts, busts, and architectural ornaments. The 1767 amendment to the Engravers' Act had made tentative gestures toward protecting casts and moulds, but its primary focus remained prints, and the extension to sculpture was uncertain in scope and had not been clearly established in practice.

The English sculptural trade in the 1790s was commercially significant. The neo-classical revival had driven demand for sculptural works of all kinds, from monumental busts and statues to decorative medallions and architectural ornaments. Josiah Wedgwood's pottery firm had pioneered the industrial production of decorative sculptural objects—the famous Wedgwood jasperware medallions, reliefs, and figures—and the success of such productions had created a substantial market for both the originals and, increasingly, unauthorized copies.

The problem of unauthorized reproduction in sculpture was in some ways more acute than in printing. A sculptor who produced an original model from which casts could be taken had no effective remedy when a competitor acquired one of the authorized casts and used it to produce further unauthorized casts. The physical reproduction of three-dimensional works was technically straightforward; the economic harm to the original sculptor could be severe; and the existing copyright statutes provided no remedy.

The **Sculpture Copyright Act 1798** was Parliament's response to this gap in protection. Its passage was driven by the combined lobbying efforts of sculptors, potters (particularly the Wedgwood firm), and manufacturers of decorative objects who had direct commercial interests in the protection of their three-dimensional designs.

---

## Scope of Protection: Original Models and Casts

The Act extended copyright protection to **original models and casts** in sculpture. The protected subject matter was defined by reference to originality and three-dimensionality: any original model or cast representing a person, animal, or other natural or artificial object was potentially protectable.

The Act specifically named the following categories of works as protected:

- **Busts** (the canonical form of sculptural portraiture)
- **Models** (three-dimensional representations of objects or persons)
- **Casts** taken from such models
- **Figures** in the round

The emphasis on "original" was consistent with the originality requirement that had characterized the Engravers' Act since 1735. A cast mechanically taken from an existing, unprotected work was not original and received no protection; only the first creative act of modeling or carving gave rise to the statutory right.

---

## Term of Protection: Fourteen Years

The Act granted a term of protection of **fourteen years** from the date of first publication or first public exhibition of the model or cast. This was consistent with the initial term established by the Statute of Anne for new books and by the Engravers' Acts for prints. Parliament was clearly treating the fourteen-year term as the standard copyright period.

Unlike the Statute of Anne, the 1798 Act provided no renewal term. At the expiry of the fourteen-year term, the work entered the public domain without any opportunity for extension. This was consistent with the print copyright regime and reflected Parliament's view that the creator's interest was adequately served by fourteen years of exclusive rights.

---

## Registration Requirement

The Act required **registration** of protected works as a precondition for bringing an action for infringement. The registration requirement served purposes similar to those it had served under the Statute of Anne and the print copyright statutes:

- It created a public record of protected works, enabling potential users to determine whether a particular work was protected.
- It fixed the date of first publication for purposes of calculating the term.
- It provided evidence in litigation of the claimant's title to the copyright.

In practice, sculptors registered their works by depositing a description or representation of the model with the Stationers' Company, which maintained the Register. The formality of registration, while potentially burdensome, was a condition of statutory protection.

---

## Significance: First Copyright Protection for Three-Dimensional Works

The Sculpture Copyright Act 1798 has a specific and distinguished place in copyright history as **the first statute to extend copyright protection to three-dimensional works**. All previous English copyright legislation had addressed two-dimensional works—books (text on paper), prints (images on paper). The 1798 Act broke with this two-dimensional tradition and recognized that creative works could be protected regardless of their dimensional form.

This was a conceptual breakthrough of lasting importance. Modern copyright law routinely protects three-dimensional works—sculptures, architectural works, product designs—on the same principles as two-dimensional works. The 1798 Act was the first legislative recognition that the criterion for copyright protection is *creative originality*, not *physical form*.

### Significance for the Industrial Arts

The Act's protection of "models and casts" rather than uniquely sculptural works gave it particular importance for the **decorative and industrial arts**. The Wedgwood pottery firm and similar manufacturers routinely produced objects in series from original models; their economic interest lay not in the uniqueness of a single sculptural object but in the right to control the production of series of identical objects from a protected model. The 1798 Act gave such producers a legal remedy against competitors who copied their models.

This extension of copyright into the industrial arts anticipated the later development of **design right** as a distinct category of intellectual property—a recognition that the aesthetic design of commercial objects deserves legal protection separate from, and on different terms from, both copyright in fine art and patent protection for functional inventions.

### Precursor to Later Expansion

The 1798 Act's protection of sculpture was the last in the sequence of eighteenth-century copyright expansions that had begun with the Statute of Anne in 1710 and continued through the Engravers' Acts of 1735, 1767, and 1777. Together, these statutes established the pattern that the nineteenth century would follow in extending copyright to dramatic works (1833), lectures (1835), paintings and drawings (1862), and ultimately all artistic works of any kind.

The eventual consolidation of all these separate category-specific statutes into the unified framework of the **Copyright Act 1911** represented the completion of a process that the Sculpture Copyright Act 1798 had materially advanced—the recognition that copyright is a general principle of intellectual property law, applicable to all original creative works, rather than a collection of trade protections specific to the book, print, and now sculptural industries.

---

## Relationship to American Law

The Sculpture Copyright Act 1798 was enacted eight years after the first US Copyright Act (1790), which had followed the Statute of Anne's model of protecting only "maps, charts, and books." The US did not extend copyright to sculpture until the Copyright Act of 1870 (16 Stat. 198), nearly three-quarters of a century after England. This gap illustrates that while England and America developed copyright law on parallel tracks derived from common sources, their legislative histories diverged significantly at the point of subject-matter expansion.

The principle established by the 1798 Act—that copyright could protect three-dimensional as well as two-dimensional works—was eventually absorbed into American copyright law and remains in force today under 17 U.S.C. § 102(a), which lists "sculptural works" among the categories of works entitled to copyright protection.

## Primary Source Text

The Sculpture Copyright Act 1798, full title *An Act for encouraging the Art of making new Models and Casts of Busts, and other Things therein mentioned*, received Royal Assent July 2, 1798.

---

**An Act for encouraging the Art of making new Models and Casts of Busts, and other Things therein mentioned**
*(38 Geo. 3 c. 71)*

**Preamble:**

> "Whereas it would contribute to the Encouragement of the Arts of making New Models and Casts, if the Artists thereof were secured in the Sole Use and Profit of their Works for such Term of Years as is herein-after mentioned..."

**Section 1 (grant of 14-year exclusive right):**

> "Be it therefore enacted by the King's most Excellent Majesty, by and with the Advice and Consent of the Lords Spiritual and Temporal, and Commons, in this present Parliament assembled, and by the Authority of the same, That from and after the passing of this Act, every Person who shall make or cause to be made any new and original Sculpture, or Model, or Copy, or Cast of the Human Figure or Human Figures, or of any Bust or Busts, or of any Part or Parts of the Human Figure, clothed in Drapery or otherwise, or of any Animal or Animals, or of any Part or Parts of any Animal combined with the Human Figure or otherwise, or of any Subject being Matter of Invention in Sculpture, or of any alto or Basso Relievo representing any of the Matters or Things herein-before mentioned, or any Cast from Nature of the Human Figure or of any Part or Parts of the Human Figure, or of any Cast from Nature of any Animal or of any Part or Parts of any Animal, shall have the sole Right and Property in all and every such new and original Sculpture, Model, Copy, and Cast, for the Term of Fourteen Years from first putting forth or publishing the same."

**Section 2 (name requirement):**

> "Provided always, That the Name of the Maker or Makers of such Sculpture, Model, Copy, or Cast, with the Date of the first Publication thereof, shall be put on all such Sculpture, Models, Copies, and Casts."

**Section 3 (penalty for infringement):**

> "That if any Person shall make or cause to be made any Piracy or unlawful Copy of any such Sculpture, Model, Copy, or Cast, during the Period herein-before mentioned, the Person so offending shall forfeit such piratical Copy, and also a Sum of Money not less than... nor more than... to be recovered by the Party aggrieved..."

## Legislative History

The 1798 Act was Parliament's direct response to the lobbying of sculptors and the Wedgwood pottery firm, who had pressed for statutory protection of three-dimensional artistic works since the 1780s. The rapid commercial expansion of the decorative arts—driven in particular by Josiah Wedgwood and Sons' mass production of jasperware medallions, plaques, and ornamental objects—created acute demand for legal protection against unauthorized copying of original models.

Josiah Wedgwood and Sons submitted evidence to Parliament on the economic harm caused by unauthorized copying of their jasperware medallion designs. The firm's jasperware line, which reproduced original sculptural models in series, was especially vulnerable to copying because a single original model could be used to produce unlimited reproductions; once a competitor obtained one legitimate specimen, it could reproduce the model indefinitely without any further reference to the original.

The bill was introduced in the House of Commons in the 1798 session (38 Geo. 3). The Commons Journals for the 38th year of George III record the bill's readings and its passage through both Houses. The bill proceeded without significant opposition and attracted parliamentary support as a natural extension of the print protection regime already established by the Engravers' Acts.

The Act passed both Houses without significant opposition and received Royal Assent on July 2, 1798.

The Act's structure deliberately mirrored that of the Engravers' Copyright Act 1735: a 14-year term of protection from first publication, a requirement that the maker's name be inscribed on the work, and a forfeiture remedy enforceable by the proprietor. This structural parallel reflected Parliament's view that sculptural works stood in the same relationship to their makers as prints stood to their engravers—creative works deserving statutory protection on similar terms.

The 1798 Act is the last major pre-1800 extension of copyright subject matter in English law, and the last in the series of acts that formed the complete pre-modern English copyright regime. Together with the Statute of Anne (1710), the Engravers' Copyright Acts (1735, 1767, 1777), and the common-law developments settled in *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774), it completed the statutory framework that the English-speaking world inherited as the foundation of modern copyright law.

The American sculpture copyright equivalent (the Copyright Act of 1870, 16 Stat. 198) would not come until 72 years later, when Congress amended the copyright statute to include works of art. This gap of nearly three-quarters of a century between English and American protection of sculptural works illustrates the divergence in pace between the two jurisdictions in expanding copyright subject matter beyond the book and print trade origins of the right.
