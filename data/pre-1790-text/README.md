# Pre-1790 English Copyright Law: Source Documents and Commentary

This directory contains Markdown files documenting the history of English copyright law and legal developments predating the first United States federal copyright statute—the Copyright Act of 1790 (1 Stat. 124). Each file covers a significant instrument, event, or decision in the development of English copyright from the establishment of the Stationers' Company monopoly in 1557 through the extension of sculptural copyright in 1798.

---

## Scope

The files cover the period **1557 to 1798**, examining:

- The guild monopoly system of the Stationers' Company (1557–1695)
- The Star Chamber decree system of press regulation (1566–1641)
- The statutory licensing regime of the Restoration (1662–1695)
- The lapse of pre-publication censorship and the resulting reform movement (1695–1710)
- The Statute of Anne, the world's first copyright statute (1710)
- The expansion of copyright to visual and three-dimensional works (1735–1798)
- The great common-law copyright controversy and its resolution (1769–1774)

---

## Types of Sources

The documents in this directory represent four distinct types of legal authority:

1. **Royal Charters** — instruments of incorporation granted by the Crown giving corporate status and regulatory powers to guilds or companies (*1557 Stationers' Company Charter*).

2. **Star Chamber Decrees** — orders of the Privy Council's court of extraordinary jurisdiction, issued on prerogative authority without parliamentary sanction (*1586 Decree*, *1637 Decree*). These were not Acts of Parliament but had the force of law until the Star Chamber was abolished in 1641.

3. **Acts of Parliament** — statutes enacted by the King-in-Parliament, which became the dominant mode of press and copyright regulation after 1641 (*1662 Licensing Act*, *1710 Statute of Anne*, *1735 Engravers' Act*, *1767 Amendment*, *1777 Prints Act*, *1798 Sculpture Act*).

4. **Common Law Decisions** — judgments of the common-law courts and the House of Lords resolving disputes between private parties, which also shaped the legal framework through interpretation of statutes and recognition (or rejection) of common-law rights (*Millar v. Taylor* 1769, *Donaldson v. Beckett* 1774).

---

## Note on Particular Importance

Two documents in this collection are of **special importance** as direct predecessors of the US Copyright Act of 1790:

### The Statute of Anne (1710)

The Statute of Anne is the immediate model for the US Copyright Act of 1790. Both statutes use the same term structure (fourteen-year initial term with a fourteen-year renewal if the author is still living), both require registration as a precondition for suit, both require deposit of copies for public libraries, and both prohibit the importation of foreign reprints. The framers of the US Constitution's Copyright Clause ("for limited Times") and the drafters of the 1790 Act worked consciously within the framework the Statute of Anne had established.

### Donaldson v. Beckett (1774)

The House of Lords' decision in *Donaldson v. Beckett* established the foundational principle that **copyright is a creature of statute, not of common law or natural right**, and that its term is fixed and limited rather than perpetual. The phrase "for limited Times" in the Copyright Clause of the US Constitution (Art. I, § 8, cl. 8) directly reflects this holding. The framers, aware of the controversy *Donaldson* had resolved, constitutionalized the limitation on term to ensure that no American Congress could grant copyright in perpetuity.

## Relationship to the US Copyright Act of 1790

The US Copyright Act of 1790 was enacted by the First Congress and signed by President Washington on May 31, 1790—eighty years after the Statute of Anne and sixteen years after *Donaldson v. Beckett*. Its structure was directly modeled on the Statute of Anne:

- **Term:** Fourteen years initial term; fourteen-year renewal if author living (identical to Statute of Anne)
- **Subject matter:** Maps, charts, and books (narrower than the Statute of Anne, which also covered prints by this date through the Engravers' Acts)
- **Registration:** Required as condition of protection, with the clerk of the district court rather than the Stationers' Company
- **Deposit:** Required copies to be deposited in the Secretary of State's office
- **Anti-importation:** Prohibited unauthorized importation of foreign reprints

The constitutional authorization for the Act—Article I, § 8, clause 8 of the US Constitution—reflects the *Donaldson v. Beckett* settlement by requiring that copyright be granted "for limited Times," foreclosing any possibility of perpetual statutory copyright on the English pre-1774 model.

The documents in this directory thus constitute the essential historical background for understanding American copyright law from its origins to the present day.
