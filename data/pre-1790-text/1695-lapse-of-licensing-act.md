# The Permanent Lapse of the Licensing Act (1695)

**Date of Expiry:** May 3, 1695  
**Parliamentary Session:** 6 & 7 Will. & Mar.  
**Significance:** End of statutory pre-publication censorship in England; catalyst for the Statute of Anne

---

## Background

The Licensing of the Press Act 1662 (14 Car. 2 c. 33) had been renewed by Parliament at irregular intervals throughout the Restoration period, with two notable lapses (1679–1685 and 1692–1693). By 1693 the Act was widely regarded as unsatisfactory by multiple constituencies: the Stationers' Company found it insufficiently protective of their monopoly against provincial and overseas reprinting; provincial booksellers resented the London monopoly it sustained; scholars objected to the delays and costs imposed by the licensing requirement; and a growing body of opinion held that perpetual monopolies over particular texts were economically and intellectually harmful.

When the Act was renewed for the last time in 1693, it was for a period of only two years. As the 1694–1695 parliamentary session progressed, both Houses considered whether and how to renew it again. A parliamentary committee in the Commons examined the Act and listed its objections. The Lords sent proposed amendments. The Commons, faced with the choice of accepting amendments it disliked or letting the Act expire, chose the latter. The Act lapsed on May 3, 1695, never to be renewed.

---

## The Parliamentary Debate

The parliamentary debate of 1694–1695 is notable for what it *did not* emphasize. Freedom of the press as an abstract libertarian principle played almost no role in the Commons' stated objections. The formal Commons objections to the Act were largely **commercial and technical**:

- The Act created a monopoly in the London book trade that injured buyers and competing sellers.
- The penalties for infringement were excessive in some cases and insufficient in others.
- The Act did not protect English booksellers against cheap reprints imported from Scotland and Ireland, which were outside the Act's effective jurisdiction.
- The definitions in the Act were uncertain and had generated litigation.
- The restriction of printing to London, Oxford, and Cambridge served the London Stationers more than the public.
- The registration system benefited the Stationers' Company at the expense of authors and the general public.

These were not the objections of principled libertarians; they were the objections of a trading nation that had grown skeptical of monopoly as an instrument of regulation. The same Parliament that let the Licensing Act expire also substantially revised the East India Company's charter and debated the regulation of other monopolistic bodies.

---

## John Locke's Memorandum on the Licensing Act

The philosophical dimension of the debate was supplied largely by **John Locke**, who prepared a memorandum for Edward Clarke, a Member of Parliament, sometime around 1693–1694. The memorandum circulated in manuscript and was closely read by the Commons committee that examined the Act.

Locke's objections were more fundamental than the parliamentary committee's technical complaints:

### Against Monopoly

Locke argued that the Stationers' Company's perpetual rights in registered copies amounted to an unjustifiable restraint of trade. A bookseller who had paid for a manuscript once—perhaps many decades ago—should not be entitled to suppress all other editions of it forever. The accumulation of "copies" by a small guild of London stationers served neither authors nor readers, but only a privileged middleman class.

### For a Limited Term

More specifically, Locke argued that *if* Parliament was to grant any exclusive right to printers and authors, it should be for a fixed and limited term—not perpetual. After that term, works should enter the public domain and be freely available to any printer. Locke suggested that fifty years was probably an adequate term, though he emphasized the point of principle over the precise number.

This argument was directly incorporated into the structure of the Statute of Anne fifteen years later. The fourteen-year initial term (with a renewal term of fourteen more years if the author was still living) that the Statute of Anne enacted is the legislative offspring of Locke's argument for a limited term.

### Against Pre-Publication Censorship

Locke argued that the requirement of pre-publication licensing was objectionable in principle, not merely in its specific implementation. The requirement that a licensor approve a book before it could be printed gave a single official the power to suppress new ideas before they could be tested in public debate. This was, Locke held, inimical to the growth of knowledge.

Locke drew a distinction between *prior restraint*—the licensing requirement—and *subsequent punishment* of genuinely harmful publications. The latter was a legitimate exercise of law; the former was a form of censorship incompatible with the free circulation of ideas. This distinction between prior restraint and subsequent liability became foundational for the English law of press freedom and was influential in the American constitutional tradition.

### Against Indefinite Terms

Locke noted that the Act had no provision for authors' rights distinct from publishers' rights. An author who assigned a manuscript to a stationer had no subsequent claim on profits from the work or any ability to control its printing. Locke thought this was unjust; the creator of a work had a natural claim to benefit from it.

## Consequences of the Lapse

### Commercial Crisis for the Book Trade

The immediate consequence of the Act's lapse was a **commercial crisis for the London Stationers**. Their registered "copies"—the texts in which they had invested capital on the assumption of perpetual monopoly—were now without clear legal protection. Any printer in England (or Scotland, or Ireland) could reproduce any book without payment or permission. Within months of the lapse, unlicensed editions of profitable works began to appear.

The Stationers could not rely on the common law to protect them, because it was unclear whether any common-law copyright existed independently of the statutory registration system. If the Licensing Act was the source of the "copy" right, then the lapse of the Act extinguished the right along with it.

### Growth of Unlicensed Printing

The lapse of the Act effectively **abolished pre-publication censorship** in England. Books, pamphlets, and newspapers could henceforth be printed without prior approval by any licensor. The press did not immediately become fully free—the common-law offenses of seditious libel, blasphemous libel, and obscene libel remained available to the government, and the treason laws applied to directly treasonous publications—but the requirement of advance approval was gone forever.

The decades following 1695 saw a marked growth in the London and provincial press. Newspapers multiplied. Political pamphlets circulated with a freedom not seen since the Civil War. The coffee-house culture of the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries—closely tied to the periodical press—flourished in the absence of licensing constraints.

### Shift in Argument: From Trade Monopoly to Author's Right

Perhaps the most important intellectual consequence of the lapse was a **shift in the terms of the argument** for press protection. As long as the Licensing Act was in force, the Stationers' Company could rely on its registered copies and the monopoly they represented. Once the Act lapsed, the Stationers needed a new argument for why Parliament should restore their rights.

They found it in the **author's natural right**. If the author of a work had a natural property right in the fruits of his or her mental labor—analogous to the laborer's right to the wages of physical labor that Locke himself had articulated—then Parliament ought to vindicate that right by statute. And if the author had such a right, the bookseller who had purchased the manuscript from the author acquired that right by assignment.

This rhetorical move was partly sincere and partly tactical. Some booksellers genuinely believed in an author's natural right; others were simply adapting their argument to the post-Licensing-Act political environment. But the move had lasting consequences: it shifted the theoretical foundation of copyright from *guild privilege* to *natural right*, and it placed the *author* at the center of the argument rather than the *publisher*. The Statute of Anne, which vested copyright initially in the author rather than the registering stationer, was the direct legislative product of this rhetorical and conceptual shift.

---

## The Petitions of 1703, 1706, 1707, and 1709

Between 1695 and 1710 the Stationers' Company and allied booksellers petitioned Parliament repeatedly for legislation to restore their rights:

- **1703:** A petition for a bill was presented but no legislation resulted.
- **1706:** A bill was introduced in the House of Commons but failed to pass.
- **1707:** Another attempt, again unsuccessful, in the year of the Acts of Union with Scotland (which created particular urgency, since Scottish printers were now in the same kingdom and even less controllable than before).
- **1709:** A bill was finally introduced that attracted serious parliamentary attention. It was shaped by the converging arguments of the Stationers (who wanted protection for their investments) and the emerging author-rights position (which demanded that authors be recognized as the initial right-holders). The resulting compromise became the **Statute of Anne**, enacted in 1710.

---

## Significance

The permanent lapse of the Licensing Act in 1695 is one of the most consequential non-events in English legal history. Its significance is threefold:

1. **It ended pre-publication censorship permanently in England.** The licensing system was never restored. Whatever controls the government imposed on the press after 1695 operated through subsequent punishment—libel law, treason law—rather than prior restraint. This was a fundamental constitutional change.

2. **It created the conceptual space for copyright.** Without the monopoly system of the Licensing Act, the Stationers needed new legal foundations. The search for those foundations produced the author-rights argument that eventually became the Statute of Anne's central innovation: copyright as a right initially vesting in the author.

3. **It prefigured the great debate in Donaldson v. Beckett (1774).** The question left open by the lapse—whether the Stationers had a perpetual common-law right in their copies independent of the statutory system—became the central question in the great copyright cases of the 1760s and 1770s. The House of Lords' answer in Donaldson (that the Statute of Anne had displaced and superseded any common-law right) traced its logic directly to the events of 1695 and the argument that copyright was a creature of statute rather than natural right.
