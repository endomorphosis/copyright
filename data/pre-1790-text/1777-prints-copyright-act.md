# The Prints Copyright Act 1777

**Citation:** 17 Geo. 3 c. 57  
**Full Title:** *An Act for more effectually securing the Property of Prints to Inventors and Engravers, by enabling them to sue for and recover Penalties in certain Cases*  
**Royal Assent:** 1777

## Background and Context

By 1777 the print protection regime established by the Engravers' Copyright Act 1735 (8 Geo. 2 c. 13) and its amendment in 1767 (7 Geo. 3 c. 38) had been in operation for over four decades. During that time the print trade had continued to expand and diversify. The engraving and etching techniques that the 1735 Act had been designed to protect were increasingly joined by a wider array of reproductive techniques, including **mezzotint**, **aquatint**, **stipple engraving**, and **lithographic processes** then in early development.

A persistent practical problem with the 1735 Act was its close identification with specific named techniques—engraving, etching—which left uncertainty about whether newer methods of print production fell within its protection. The 1767 amendment had addressed this partially by referring to works "of a similar nature," but the question remained contested. Infringers routinely argued that a particular print was not an "engraving" or "etching" in the technical sense and therefore fell outside the statute.

The more fundamental problem was the formality requirement. Under the 1735 Act, a print was only protected if the designer's name and the date of first publication were **engraved on the plate**. If the plate bore no such inscription, the print was unprotected regardless of its artistic merit or commercial value. This requirement placed a significant burden on printmakers and led to forfeiture of protection through inadvertent omission.

The 1777 Act addressed both the technique-specificity problem and the formality problem, producing a more comprehensive and practically effective protection for the print trade.

---

## Extension to All Prints Regardless of Technique

The most significant substantive provision of the 1777 Act was its extension of copyright protection to **all prints, regardless of the technique by which they were produced**. The Act dropped the reference to specific techniques (engraving, etching) and instead protected any "print" as such, whether produced by engraving, etching, mezzotint, aquatint, stipple, or any other technique.

This change was conceptually important. It marked a shift from **technique-based** to **work-based** definition of the protected subject matter. What mattered was the product—the print—not the process by which it was made. This work-based approach is now standard in copyright law: we protect films, not cinematographic processes; we protect sound recordings, not particular recording technologies. The 1777 Act was an early move in that direction.

The effect was immediate: printmakers using newer reproductive techniques could now clearly rely on the statutory protection without the uncertainty that had plagued the years since 1735. Mezzotint and stipple engravers in particular benefited, as their techniques had been most doubtful under the original Act.

---

## Requirement to Engrave Name and Date

The 1777 Act retained the requirement of the 1735 Act that the **designer's or proprietor's name and the date of first publication be engraved** (or otherwise inscribed) on the plate or other means of reproduction. This inscription served as the notice to the world that the print was protected and identified who held the rights.

However, the Act specified that the inscription had to be on the **plate or block or other means of reproduction from which the print was made**, rather than merely on the printed copies themselves. This clarification was important: it tied the notice requirement to the productive apparatus rather than to individual copies, which might bear imperfect or partially legible impressions.

The formality requirement remained a trap for unwary printmakers: failure to inscribe the plate forfeited statutory protection. But the 1777 Act's clarification of what the inscription must say and where it must appear reduced the frequency of accidental forfeiture by inadvertent non-compliance.

---

## Twenty-Eight Day Registration Requirement

A new provision introduced by the 1777 Act was a **twenty-eight-day registration requirement**. The printmaker was required to make an entry of the print with a designated officer—in practice, with the Stationers' Company—within twenty-eight days of first publication. Failure to register within this period forfeited the right to bring an action for infringement, though it did not necessarily void the underlying right.

This time-limited registration requirement was designed to prevent the difficulties that arose when old prints with uncertain provenance were claimed as protected. By requiring prompt registration, the Act created a current and reliable record of protected prints. The twenty-eight-day window was tight enough to require genuine attention without being so short as to make compliance practically impossible.

The registration-within-a-time-limit concept anticipated the modern copyright registration systems of several jurisdictions, including the United States, where registration within a specified period is required to bring certain types of infringement actions.

---

## Increased Penalties

The 1777 Act substantially **increased the penalties** for infringement of print copyright beyond those available under the 1735 Act. The escalation reflected both general inflation since 1735 and Parliament's recognition that the print trade had grown large enough for infringing editions to represent serious commercial harm.

The penalty structure under the 1777 Act included:

- **Forfeiture of all infringing copies** to the proprietor, as before.
- **Forfeiture of the plates or other means of reproduction** used to make the infringing copies, as before.
- An increased **per-copy monetary penalty** payable to the proprietor, significantly higher than the original Act's provision.
- A **minimum total penalty** per infringement, regardless of the number of copies, ensuring that even small-scale infringement was not economically trivial to punish.

The structure of split penalties—part to the Crown, part to the complainant—was carried over from the Statute of Anne and the 1735 Act. This qui tam structure (allowing private parties to bring actions partly in the public interest) was the standard eighteenth-century mechanism for encouraging enforcement of regulatory statutes without the need for a professional public prosecutor.

---

## Relationship to Concurrent Legal Developments

The 1777 Act was enacted three years after the House of Lords' landmark decision in *Donaldson v. Beckett* (1774), which had resolved the debate about perpetual common-law copyright in favour of the statutory model. The practical and conceptual environment in which the 1777 Act was drafted was therefore one in which copyright was understood as a creature of statute—Parliament's grant—rather than as a natural right.

The 1777 Act's technique-agnostic definition of the protected subject matter (all prints, regardless of how made) is consistent with this understanding: Parliament was defining the scope of its statutory grant, and it chose to define it functionally rather than technically. This approach was more durable than the technique-specific approach of the 1735 Act and anticipated the broad subject-matter definitions now characteristic of major copyright statutes.

---

## Significance

The Prints Copyright Act 1777 has three principal claims to importance in copyright history:

1. **It completed the transformation of print copyright from technique-specific to work-specific protection.** By protecting all prints regardless of technique, it established the principle that copyright protects the creative *work* rather than the particular *process* by which it is reproduced. This principle is foundational in modern copyright law.

2. **It introduced the time-limited registration requirement**, which anticipated modern registration deadlines and created a more reliable and current register of protected works.

3. **It increased penalties to levels commensurate with the commercial stakes of the print trade**, demonstrating Parliament's willingness to calibrate copyright enforcement mechanisms to the economic realities of the industries protected. The 1777 Act's penalty structure was the model for penalty provisions in subsequent copyright statutes through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

Together with the 1735 and 1767 Acts, the 1777 Act completed the statutory framework for print copyright that remained in force until the Copyright Act 1911 consolidated all copyright into a single statute for the first time.

**An Act for more effectually securing the Property of Prints to Inventors and Engravers, by enabling them to sue for and recover Penalties in certain Cases**
*(17 Geo. 3 c. 57)*

**Preamble:**

> "Whereas the Act made in the Eighth Year of the Reign of His late Majesty King George the Second, for the Encouragement of the Arts of Designing, Engraving, and Etching Historical and other Prints, and the Act made in the Seventh Year of the Reign of His present Majesty to amend and render more effectual the said Act, have not been found sufficient for the protection of such Works; and it is expedient to make further Provision for enabling the Proprietors of such Works to recover Penalties..."

**Section 1 (extension to all prints):**

> "Be it enacted by the King's most Excellent Majesty, by and with the Advice and Consent of the Lords Spiritual and Temporal, and Commons, in this present Parliament assembled, and by the Authority of the same, That every Person who shall engrave, etch, or work in Mezzotinto or Chiaro Oscuro, or cause to be engraved, etched, or worked, any Portrait, Conversation, Landscape, or Architecture, Map, Chart, Plan, or any other Print or Prints whatsoever, shall have the sole Right and Liberty of Printing and Reprinting the same for the Term of Twenty-eight Days from the Day of the first Publishing thereof..."

**Section 2 (name and date requirement):**

> "Provided always, That no Person shall be entitled to the Benefit of this Act, unless the Name and Place of Abode of the Inventor, Designer, Engraver, or Proprietor shall be truly inscribed on the said Print or Prints, with the Day of the first Publication thereof."

**Section 3 (penalty for infringement):**

> "That if any Person shall engrave, etch, or otherwise copy in any Size, or shall import into Great Britain, or shall sell or expose to Sale, any such Print, without the Consent of the Proprietor or Proprietors, first had and obtained in Writing under the Hand of such Proprietor or Proprietors, in the Presence of Two or more Credible Witnesses, such Offender or Offenders shall forfeit the Plate or Plates on which such Print or Prints were copied, and all and every Sheet or Sheets of the same, to the Proprietor or Proprietors of the original Print or Prints..."

## Legislative History

By the mid-1770s the print trade had continued to expand and both the Engravers' Copyright Act 1735 (8 Geo. 2 c. 13) and its 1767 amendment (7 Geo. 3 c. 38) were seen as inadequate in two respects: in scope (the earlier acts were tied to specific engraving techniques and did not cover all methods of print reproduction) and in the speed of their remedies (the existing mechanisms for recovering penalties were slow and cumbersome relative to the pace of commercial infringement).

The bill was introduced in the House of Commons during the 17th Parliament's session of 1777. The Commons Journals for 17 Geo. 3 record the bill's first, second, and third readings, its committee stage, and its passage without significant amendment.

The Act passed without significant opposition, as there was a broad cross-party consensus that print protection was commercially necessary. The expanding print trade—driven by the growth of illustrated publications, satirical prints, and the market for architectural and cartographic works—gave all interested parties a strong incentive to support legislative reinforcement of the protection regime.

The 1777 Act is notable for extending protection to all prints regardless of technique, moving from a technique-based to a work-based definition of the protected subject matter. Where the 1735 Act had protected only engravings, etchings, and works in mezzotinto, the 1777 Act applied to "any other Print or Prints whatsoever," ensuring that new reproductive techniques would not fall outside the statute's scope.

The Act received Royal Assent in 1777, completing the legislative sequence that had begun with the 1735 Act.

The 28-day registration window—requiring that the proprietor's name and the date of first publication be inscribed on the print within 28 days of first publishing—was a new procedural feature designed to make the formality requirement more flexible than the immediate pre-publication registration required under earlier statutes. It acknowledged the practical realities of the print trade while preserving the principle that registration was a condition of statutory protection.
